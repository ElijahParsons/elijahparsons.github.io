Briefcase Bass Amp

The goal of this project is to create a convenient solution for musicians to keep all their gear in one place. This led to the convenient solution of a single briefcase that contains the bass amp and speaker, cords, and multi fx.

ROLE Designer, Fabricater
DATE 2026
TOOLS Onshape, 3D printing, Woodworking, Wiring

The Problem

When a musician is carrying their gear they only have two available hands and one of them is already taken by carrying the bass guitar. This leaves only one hand to carry everything else that they need. This means that the entire musical setup needs to be contained under one handle.

The road of one taking one trip from the car to the playing location led to the idea of a briefcase amp. A briefcase that contained the whole setup where the only thing that needed to be done is open it and plug it in and its ready to play.

Engineering Goals

The goal for this project was exactly what was explained above; to create a plug and play musical setup for bassists. The vision for this convenient setup required the following criteria:

  • Everything is setup when you open up the case
  • Speaker big enough to play with a live drummer
  • Multi fx and amp head are secure but can easily be removed without permanent changes

Overall Design Choice

For a desk project of this size the most efficient way to get physical parts is through 3D printing. The robot was designed for 3D printing for framing and supports with friction fit holes for bearings, gears and screws. To get the parts screwed together, brass threaded inserts were chosen for a strong reliable mounts for screws. To compute the speech to text and live video feedback, the computing power of a raspberry pi was needed. To drive the Servos a hat was chosen to attach to the raspberry pi for the ease of connection and shared language.

The option of routing the computing through a computer would have been cheaper but it was ultimately decided that keeping the computing local was worth the extra price. Using an arduino was also in the decision making due to its popularity and large amount of online advice and help, however the languages would have conflicted with the raspberry pi which would have most likely taken more time to figure out than the online help would save which is why a hat directly compatible with the raspberry pi was chosen.

3D Modeling and Design

The 3D model was modeled in its entirety using Onshape. Each arm segment had its own assembly, including the printed parts, bearings, gears, and servos. Each of these individual subassemblies were then assembled into one master assembly to show the entire robot arm and its functionality. The master assembly shows mating interactions to simulate the expected movement of the arm.

Some design decisions that were made through the modeling process were to place the servos closer to the base than the joint they are controlling to reduce the moment of the arm when it is stretched out. The servos are connected to 3D printed gears to attach to GT2 belts that attach to the joints. The grabber has a complicated pulley system that ultimately did not work after testing and different approaches are in progress.

Printing and Assembling the Arm

The parts were all printed and the threaded inserts were placed into the parts as needed. The arm was then assembled with all the bearings, belts, parts, screws, and servos.

All the parts fit together as expected from the 3D model however there were some parts that were difficult to reach the screws that connect them. This required some of the parts to be sanded down or drilled through in order to reach the necessary screw.

Servos and Raspberry Pi setup

The servos need power and a brain. The hat that was chosen cannot have 10 amps of 6V current run through it at once which is the power of all servos at max power at once. To solve this issue the power supply was cut and spliced directly to the servo wires to power the servos directly without going through the board. The signal wires were then ran to the board so the board could still communicate with the servos. The ribbon cable that connects the raspberry pi to the hat also had the wires going to the wrong locations so the wires needed to be rerouted in order for the raspberry pi to effectively communicate with the hat.

These modifications all work as intended and the raspberry pi contains scripts to move the servos which are correctly powered outside of the hat.

Coding the Robot

The electrical components work as intended and the raspberry pi can effectively communicate with all of the servos. Scripts have been written that can control and test each servo that is mounted on the robot arm, however the structural integrity of the robot has not been established enough to move much further in the coding part of this project.

These scripts allow individual testing of each servo and how the arm behaves and interacts with each movement. This is very helpful in the iterative testing portion of this project because it makes problems easy to diagnose which will speed up progress of the project.

Final Result and Lessons Learned

The final result is a structurally sound robot arm and a safe servo system that can easily be coded and tested. The belt on the shoulder slips but all other joints work well. Potential decisions moving forward might include mounting the servo directly to remove the possibility to slip or purchase manufactured GT2 belts to prevent slipping.

Changes are currently being pursued for the shoulder and the grabber. I have learned about how motor controllers, servos, and raspberry pis work through this project and how to apply them to a moving mechanical system. I am continuing to deepen my understanding of the iterative process of rapid prototyping and what it takes to complete a project to complete a goal.
